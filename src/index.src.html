Aunt Hillary

Aunt Hillary is a made-up character from Douglas Hofstadter's book Gödel, Escher, Bach: a whole ant colony who can talk. No single ant understands a word, but the colony as a whole does. Here an AI plays her, and you are her old friend, the Anteater.

Is she real?

No. Real ant colonies don't talk or think in words. But scientists do call a colony a superorganism, because thousands of ants, each following simple rules, work together like one living thing. They find food, share it and defend the nest with no ant in charge. Even the queen doesn't give orders; her job is laying eggs.

Aunt Hillary is a way of imagining what the whole colony's point of view might be like. Hofstadter used her to think about how a mind could come from lots of small parts that don't understand anything themselves, like the cells in your brain.

The ants here are much simpler than real ones. Real ants use many different smells and touches to send messages, and a real colony doesn't starve in a few minutes.

This is everything the AI playing Aunt Hillary is told about the colony right now. It changes as the colony does, and she is told nothing else about what's on the screen.

Guess first, then try:

  • Turn the scent off. What happens to the trails? Does the pantry still fill?
  • Make the scent last 60 seconds, then scatter the middle of a trail. Does the colony find the food again sooner?
  • Let ants smell food from 8 ant-lengths away. Do they still need trails?
  • Make the scent spread a lot. Are the trails better or worse at guiding ants?
  • With the scent off, how close to the nest must a pile be for the colony to live on it?

Every ant is the same top-down drawing, turned to face where it walks. It steps through the walking frames as it moves and switches to the carrying frames when it picks up a crumb. Laden ants leave the golden scent trail that the others follow back to the food. Every crumb carried home goes in the pantry, and every ant eats from it. When there's food to spare the queen lays new ants, which crawl out of the nest, and each egg costs food, so the colony grows with plenty and shrinks when ants starve or are eaten. Move the Ants slider to call for more ants or wish some away. Open "The ants' rules" to change how the ants behave. Time speeds everything up or slows it down together; Walking speed changes only how fast the creatures walk, so faster walkers bring home more food than the colony eats. When the pantry runs dry the ants slow down, the queen stops laying, ants begin to starve, and Aunt Hillary asks you for help. How Ant Playground was made · Ken Kahn's post about it.

Set up an experiment

Run the same colony many times, changing one thing at a time, and see what difference it makes. Each run is a fresh colony on its own patch of ground, with the nest in the middle and the food in a ring around it. It runs fast and out of sight, and the ants follow the same rules as in the Colony.

Everything else stays the same in every run (open to change it)

  • All the walkers are drawn from straight above with the head up, so one row turns to face any of 360 directions.
  • A pile shows one of four stages depending on how much is left. Sprinkled crumbs and dropped ones use the single crumbs.
  • Each frame is pinned on the tip of the abdomen and scaled by its width, so the body holds still while the legs move.
  • The carrying ant was drawn darker than the walking one. Its body is tinted to match; the crumb is left alone.
  • In the colony, frames advance with distance walked rather than time, so the feet keep pace at any speed.
